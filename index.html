Life sciences industry = companies in pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, medical devices, biomedical technologies, nutraceuticals, cosmeceuticals, food processing & others that make products to improve the lives of organisms.

3. Regulatory requirements for a pharma plant

When designing a pharma plant, requirements come from 2 categories:

  1. cGMP guidelines (international)
  2. Factories Act & Rules (central act; rules made by each state — notes refer to Maharashtra)

+ separate rules on water/air pollution & storage of inflammable materials.

cGMP requirements are grouped as: (a) surroundings, (b) general requirements, (c) departmental areas, (d) special products (e.g., sterile).

(a) Surroundings — Schedule M, Part I

Factory must be located to avoid contamination from open sewage, drains, public lavatories, or factories producing obnoxious odour, fumes, soot, dust, smoke, chemicals or biological emissions. (Only Indian cGMP talks about "surroundings".)

Site selection factors (industrial zones like Baddi, Himachal Pradesh): land cost, water, electric power, climate extremes, earthquake-proneness, transport, skilled manpower, labour laws, cost of living.

(b) General requirements (summary of 10 points)

  1. Plant located, designed, built & maintained to suit operations.
  2. Layout minimises errors, allows cleaning → avoid cross-contamination & dust build-up. (Canadian guideline adds "orderliness" — "ultimate objective is product quality".)
  3. HVAC planned carefully: temperature, humidity, differential pressure, air changes/hour, class of air; separate AHU so only one product exposed per area. (Seven-zone concept — Cadila's Dholka plant was a pioneer.)
  4. Can always be kept clean & sanitised — central vacuum lines, trash points (bags collected ≥ twice per 8-h shift). Aseptic areas: vacuum cleaners of SS 304 + HEPA. USFDA treats sanitary supervisor as a key post.
  5. Kept in good repair; maintenance without affecting product (service lanes).
  6. SOPs + records for cleaning, maintenance, disinfection, sanitisation.
  7. Maintain electric supply, lighting, temperature, humidity, HVAC — no peeling paint, no broken tiles, fix leaks/drains, wiring, doors & interlocks, lights, lawns (insects!).
  8. Pest control — keep out insects, rodents, birds; toxic baits controlled.
  9. No unauthorised entry — esp. production, stores, QC.
  10. Waste removed continually; written sanitation procedures; responsibility assigned in writing.

(c) Departmental areas

AreaKey requirement
Storage & weighingenough capacity for orderly storage of starting/packaging material, intermediates, bulk, finished, quarantine, released, rejected, returned, recalled; clean, dry, within temperature limits; special conditions monitored
Productiondedicated, self-contained facilities for highly sensitising materials (penicillin) or biologicals (live microbes); some antibiotics, hormones, cytotoxics not in same facility
QC labsseparate from production; biological, microbiological & radioisotope labs separated from each other with separate AHUs; space to avoid mix-ups; separate instrument room (vibration, electrical interference, moisture)
Sterile productssee list below 👇
Ancillaryrest/refreshment rooms separate; toilets not opening directly into production/storage; maintenance workshops separate; animal houses isolated with separate entrance & AHU

Sterile products area — rules ★

  • Grade B areas designed so all operations can be observed from outside.
  • Surfaces smooth, impervious, unbroken; no uncleanable recesses, few ledges/shelves; sliding doors undesirable.
  • False ceilings sealed; pipes/ducts not creating recesses.
  • Sinks & drains avoided — excluded from aseptic areas.
  • Changing rooms designed as airlocks; hand washing only in the first stage.
  • Airlock doors never opened simultaneously → interlock + visual/audible alarm.
  • Positive pressure vs. lower grade; adjacent rooms of different grades: 10–15 pascals differential.
  • Warning system for air-supply failure; pressure indicators recorded.
  • Two categories of sterile manufacture: terminally sterilised vs. aseptically prepared (Annex 1, "Orange Guide").

4. GMP — Good Manufacturing Practice

GMP = a (quality assurance) system ensuring products (food, cosmetics, pharma) are consistently produced & controlled according to set quality standards.

Guards against: cross-contamination, adulteration, mislabelling. Benefits → cuts losses & waste, avoids recalls, fines & jail time; protects company and consumer.

Areas GMP covers: quality management · sanitation & hygiene · building & facilities · equipment · raw materials · personnel · validation & qualification · complaints · documentation & record-keeping · inspections & quality audits.

GMP vs cGMP

GMPcGMP (current GMP)
Whatbasic regulation by US FDA under the Federal Food, Drug & Cosmetic ActFDA's push for continuous improvement
Focusproactive steps → safe & effective productsup-to-date systems & technology, risk management
Features—automation, electronic documentation, risk-based QM, data integrity, continuous monitoring, process optimisation

In most cases the two terms are used interchangeably.

10 principles of GMP

  2. Enforce / implement SOPs & work instructions
  3. Document procedures & processes
  4. Validate effectiveness of SOPs
  5. Design & use working systems
  6. Maintain systems, facilities, equipment
  7. Develop job competence of workers
  8. Prevent contamination through cleanliness
  9. Prioritise quality, integrate into workflow
  10. Conduct GMP audits regularly

4 measures to uphold GMP

  4. Compliance training

FDA inspects facilities for CGMP compliance → serious violation = recall of all products. A GMP audit improves: buildings & facilities, materials mgmt, QC systems, manufacturing, packaging & labelling, QMS, personnel & training, purchasing, automation, customer service.

6. Prospects in the life science industry

One of the fastest-growing sectors — studies living organisms to improve health, agriculture, food & environment.

  • Healthcare demand ↑ — population, ageing, chronic disease (cancer, diabetes, CVD).
  • Biopharmaceuticals — vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, recombinant proteins, gene & stem-cell therapy; safer, more targeted.
  • Personalised/precision medicine — uses genetic profile, biomarkers, lifestyle → fewer side effects.
  • AI & digital health — faster drug discovery, diagnosis, imaging; wearables, big data.
  • Genomics & bioinformatics — DNA sequencing → disease mechanisms, drug targets.
  • Vaccines — mRNA, DNA, cancer vaccines (COVID-19 showed need); regenerative medicine — stem cells, tissue engineering.
  • Agri/environmental — GM crops, biofertilisers, biopesticides; bioremediation, biofuels.
  • Industrial biotech — enzymes, bioplastics; jobs in upstream/downstream, QA, QC, GMP.
  • Clinical research & RA — pharmacovigilance, clinical data mgmt, regulatory affairs.

Careers: research scientist, microbiologist, molecular biologist, biotechnologist, bioinformatician, CRA, QA/QC officer, regulatory affairs specialist, production executive, medical writer, bioprocess engineer.

7. Role of a QC Chemist & QC Microbiologist

QC Microbiologist 🦠

  • ensures microbes in product/environment meet safety & quality standards
  • tests raw materials, in-process & finished products → free from harmful microbes, meet regulatory specs
  • maintains cleanliness & sanitation of production area
  • develops/implements QC procedures
  • needs: microbiology + lab techniques + regulations
  • qualification: bachelor's in microbiology (+ lab experience); master's sometimes; ASM's CMLP certification helps

QC Chemist ⚗️

  • tests raw materials, in-process & finished products for quality, safety, regulatory standards
  • uses HPLC, GC, UV-Vis, FTIR, titration
  • works with QA, Production, R&D, Regulatory → GMP/GLP compliance
  • calibrates equipment, maintains documentation, follows SOPs, validations
  • stability studies & environmental monitoring
  • develops & validates analytical methods; trends QC data
  • takes part in internal & external audits

Skills for QC chemist: GLP familiarity, HPLC/GC, statistical software, regulatory knowledge (FDA, ISO), B.Sc. Chemistry/Biochem, attention to detail, communication.

1. Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)

GLP = a quality system concerned with organisation & management of labs, personnel responsibilities, facilities & equipment, SOPs, documentation & record-keeping, QA & data integrity.

GLP gives a framework within which lab studies are:

Objectives of GLP

  • reliable & reproducible results
  • minimise errors & contamination
  • traceability of lab activities
  • proper documentation
  • support regulatory compliance
  • protect lab personnel & samples

Principles of GLP

  • facilities provided by organisation
  • efficient training of personnel
  • validated equipment / reagents
  • SOPs, process validation
  • correctness of results
  • recorded & documented results

Key elements of GLP

  1. qualified & trained personnel
  2. suitable lab facilities
  3. calibrated & maintained equipment
  4. validated / verified test methods
  5. SOPs
  6. proper sample handling
  7. documentation & records
  8. QC & QA
  9. waste management & biosafety

Why GLP in a microbiology lab? 🧫

aseptic techniques · environmental monitoring · media preparation & QC · sterilisation · culture handling · prevention of cross-contamination · proper labelling & storage · recording observations immediately

2. Pharmacopoeial guidelines

Pharmacopoeia = an officially recognised compendium (official collection) of standards for drugs, excipients, dosage forms & analytical procedures. Sets legally accepted specs for identity, purity, strength, quality, storage, packaging, labelling & testing.
PharmacopoeiaPublished by / for
IP – Indian PharmacopoeiaIndian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC); official for drugs marketed in India. 10th edition released in 2026
BP – British Pharmacopoeiaofficial for the UK
USP–NF – US Pharmacopeia–National Formularydrugs & excipients in the USA
Ph. Eur. – European Pharmacopoeiaharmonised for European countries
Ph. Int. – International PharmacopoeiaWHO, for global public health

Objectives

uniform quality standards · ensure identity, purity, potency, safety · prevent substandard / adulterated medicines · provide validated analytical methods · support approval & GMP · national/international harmonisation.

Main components

Analytical methods must be validated for: Accuracy · Precision · Specificity · Linearity · Range · Robustness (trick: "A P S L R R")

Advantages
  • batch-to-batch consistency
  • patient safety
  • supports GMP
  • helps registration & export
  • standard analytical methods
Limitations
  • different pharmacopoeias → different limits/methods
  • new drugs may lack a monograph
  • in-house specs may still be needed

Pharmacopoeial standards are legally enforceable — e.g., medicines labelled "IP" must comply with IP.

5. Process validation — IQ, OQ, PQ

FDA: process validation = "collection & evaluation of data, from process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product."

IQ checks

  • package vs packing list
  • damage check
  • right location
  • connections & ancillary equipment
  • power supply
  • environment matches mfr. specs
  • calibration dates recorded
  • manuals & certificates organised

OQ tests

  • displays, LEDs, signals
  • temperature controls
  • humidity control
  • fan/motor RPM
  • servo motors, air flaps
  • card access
  • timers & triggers
  • upper & lower limits, alarms

PQ protocol (FDA)

  • mfg conditions, parameters, raw materials
  • data & evaluation plan
  • tests + acceptance criteria
  • sampling plan (within/between batches)
  • science- & risk-based criteria
  • facility & personnel training
  • validated analytical methods
  • approval by QA & all depts.
  • Each phase controlled by an approved protocol: objective, equipment, data to collect, acceptance criteria (pass/fail), deviation handling.
  • When needed? (21 CFR 820) — when result cannot be fully verified by later inspection: welding, soldering, painting, heat treating, sterile package sealing (checking would mean opening = destroying it), injection moulding, aseptic filling.
  • Re-qualification triggers: relocation, major repair, new tooling, software/firmware change, utility change, calibration failure, repeated deviations, data showing loss of control.
  • Roles: process owner/sponsor (need), validation lead (protocol, execution, report), equipment owner (installation & operation), QA approves protocol & evidence, IT for software/data integrity.
  • Software validation today → risk-based Computer Software Assurance (CSA).

1. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

PPE = equipment or clothing designed to protect workers from workplace hazards. PPE is the last line of defence — after engineering & administrative controls.

Hazards PPE protects from:

  • biological
  • chemical
  • radiological
  • electrical
  • mechanical

Key steps: proper selection, fitting, use, cleaning & disposal. Without PPE → injuries & illness.

Chemical & nuclear PPE — OSHA HAZMAT levels ★

OSHA = Occupational Safety & Health Administration (workplace safety). CDC = Centers for Disease Control & Prevention (infection control). For chemicals → refer to MSDS.

CBRN zones (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, Nuclear):
  • Red = highest contamination → Level A, SCBA, strict entry/exit
  • Yellow = moderate → Level B or C (based on air monitoring, skin risk, proximity)
  • Green = lowest → standard precautions, keep monitoring
Always: decontaminate & dispose used PPE properly.
Takeaways — PPE must be… selected carefully · checked before use · fit & worn correctly · removed correctly · stored & maintained · removed if contaminated · decontaminated before reuse/disposal

2. Clean room classifications & requirements

Cleanroom classification = level of cleanliness of a controlled environment measured by the number of airborne particles per m³ of air.
ISO 14644-1 definition: "a room with control of particulates and set environmental parameters… classification is set by the cleanliness of the air."

In a cleanroom: airborne particles controlled · microbial contamination minimised · temperature & humidity controlled · pressure differentials maintained · systematic cleaning & disinfection.

Important in: pharma & sterile drug mfg, biotech, micro labs, cell culture, vaccines, medical devices, food & nutraceuticals, biochem/molecular biology labs.

Sources of contamination

Biggest source = PERSONNEL (people)

Others: air, raw materials, equipment, water, surfaces, cleaning materials, improper movement, poor gowning.

Humans continuously release: skin flakes, hair, respiratory droplets, microorganisms, dust particles.

3 classification systems

(a) ISO (International Organization for Standardization) — ISO 14644-1 · (b) EU GMP grades (Annex 1) — A, B, C, D · (c) FDA / Federal Standard 209E — Class 100… (old, replaced by ISO).

3. Environmental monitoring (EM) & clean room practices

Environmental monitoring = systematic assessment of cleanroom conditions to ensure the area stays within specified limits and does not compromise product/sample quality.
Also: "the qualified system for sampling process areas to ensure they operate within defined parameters." Samples come from air, surfaces, components, utilities & personnel.

A cleanroom controls: airborne particles · microorganisms · temperature · relative humidity · air pressure.

Media & incubation

  • Target: mesophilic bacteria & fungi (grow 20–35 °C).

  • Media validated with < 100 CFU challenge (B. subtilis, S. aureus, C. albicans, P. aeruginosa, A. niger + environmental isolates); growth promotion on every lot; add neutralisers where disinfectant residues are expected.
  • Read with artificial light; extrapolate non-standard samples (not 1 m³, not 4 h).

Module IV — Basic Laboratory Instruments

1. Chromatography — the basics

Chromatography = separation based on differential distribution of components between a stationary phase and a mobile phase. (Interaction of stationary phase + mobile phase + analyte.)

History: Michael Tswett (Russian biochemist), 1906 — separated chlorophyll/plant pigments; each band had a distinct colour. Greek: chroma = colour, graphein = to write.

  • Two mutually immiscible phases are brought in contact.
  • Sample in mobile phase undergoes repeated interactions (partitions) between phases.
  • Components differ slightly in physico-chemical properties → migrate at different rates → separate.

Partition (distribution) coefficient, K

K = Cs / Cm
Cs = conc. of solute in stationary phase · Cm = conc. in mobile phase
  • High K → more in stationary → slower
  • Low K → more in mobile → faster
  • K ≈ 1 → equal in both
Solve:Cs = 8 mg/mL, Cm = 2 mg/mL → K = 8/2 = 4 → high K → compound stays in stationary phase & moves slowly.

Types of chromatography

By separation mechanism:
  1. Adsorption
  2. Partition
  3. Ion-exchange
  4. Size-exclusion
  5. Affinity
By physical format:
  • Planar (paper, TLC) → fixed time, measure distance
  • Column (e.g., HPLC) → fixed distance, measure time

2. Column chromatography

Column chromatography separates & purifies mixture components on a vertical column packed with stationary phase, based on differential affinity. Mobile phase moves by gravity or air pressure.

Basic components

  1. Sample/analyte — mixture to separate
  2. Column — vertical tube packed with stationary phase. Stationary phase (adsorbent): silica gel, alumina, ion-exchange resins, polymer resins, cellulose
  3. Mobile phase — solvent (liquid or gas): hexane, methanol, water, chloroform
  4. Pump — controls flow rate · Injector — puts sample on top
  5. Detector — identifies & quantifies · Fraction collector — collects separated compounds

Column packing: Dry method (powdered stationary phase) or Wet method (slurry = stationary + mobile phase). Cotton/glass-wool plug at bottom.

3. Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)

TLC = separation/identification of a mixture using a finely divided adsorbent (solid/liquid) spread over a plate (stationary phase) and a liquid mobile phase. A planar technique; separation by adsorption, partition, or both.
Rf = distance travelled by solute ÷ distance travelled by solvent front
(both measured from the origin/baseline) · Rf = Retention / Retardation factor · e.g. 4 cm / 8 cm = 0.5 · Rf is always between 0 and 1
  1. Stationary phase — thin adsorbent layer on glass, plastic or aluminium foil: silica gel, alumina (Al₂O₃), cellulose.
  2. Mobile phase — solvent/mixture moves up by capillary action.
  3. Separation — higher affinity for stationary → slower; lower affinity → faster → separate spots.
  4. Visualisation — UV light, iodine chamber, chemical staining reagents.

Applications: monitor reaction progress · identify compounds in mixture · check purity · ceramides & fatty acids · pesticides/insecticides in food & water · dye composition of fibres (forensics).

Pros
  • simple, short development time
  • spots easy to see
  • isolates most compounds
  • fast, high selectivity
Cons
  • can't separate enantiomers & some isomers
  • need known Rf values to identify
  • short stationary phase → limited separation length

4. High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)

HPLC = a liquid chromatography method to separate & analyse components of liquid mixtures — a modified column chromatography using high pressure to pump liquid through a column of very small stationary-phase particles. First commercial system: Waters Associates, 1967.

Process: Sample injection → mobile phase → column → separation → detector → chromatogram

Instrumentation

  1. Solvent reservoir — stores mobile phase (acetonitrile, methanol, water)
  2. Pump — constant flow at high pressure
  3. Degasser — removes dissolved gases (bubbles → pressure fluctuation, pump problems, noise)
  4. Sample injector — precise volume without disturbing flow
  5. Column + oven — fine particles, SS tube; silica or polymer; guard column protects; oven controls temperature
  6. Detector — UV-Vis, fluorescence, refractive index (RI), ELSD, mass spec (MS)
  7. Data system — controls instrument, makes chromatogram
  • Retention time (tR) → identification; peak area → concentration/quantity.
  • Strong stationary interaction → longer retention; weak → shorter.
  • Gradient elution = changing composition of solvents A & B over time (T1–T8) → better detection, shorter analysis.

5. Gas Chromatography (GC)

GC (gas–liquid) separates volatile compounds based on boiling point + interaction with stationary phase.
Mobile phase = inert carrier gas (helium) · Stationary phase = high-boiling liquid / waxy polymer adsorbed on diatomaceous earth · coiled column ~4 m · oven 50–250 °C.
  • Each peak = one component; identify by retention time & peak size.
  • Very accurate: picomoles in 1 mL liquid, ppb in gases.
  • Applications: airborne pollutants · performance-enhancing drugs in athletes' urine · oil spills · essential oils in perfumes · toxic substances in soil/air/water · forensics (arson, paint chips, toxicology, drug doses).
Absorption vs AdsorptionAbsorption = taken into the bulk (inside the sponge). Adsorption = sticks on the surface. GC/TLC stationary phases work by adsorption on a surface.

Types of GC injectors (capillary columns)

  1. Split injection — needle valve splits sample into 2 unequal flows; small part → column, big part vented. Not for highest sensitivity.
  2. Splitless — all sample enters column; sample must be very dilute; high-capacity columns (SCOT / heavily coated WCOT).
  3. On-column — fine quartz needle; cooled ~20 °C below b.p., then warmed to vaporise.
  4. Automatic (autosampler) — reproducible, many samples, no attendant; solids as solution or sealed ampoule crushed in gas stream.

6. UV-Visible spectroscopy

UV-Vis spectroscopy measures how much of discrete wavelengths of UV/visible light are absorbed by or transmitted through a sample compared to a reference/blank. Tells what is in a sample and at what concentration.
  • Energy ∝ 1/wavelength → short wavelength = high energy = high frequency.
  • A specific energy promotes electrons to a higher energy state → seen as absorption. Different bonding environments need different energies → substances absorb at different wavelengths.
  • Range: 190–800 nm → UV 190–400 nm, visible 400–800 nm.
Transmittance: T = I / I₀
I = light through sample, I₀ = light through blank
Absorbance (optical density, OD): A = –log T
Beer–Lambert law
A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = ε l c
ε = molar absorptivity · l = path length (cm) · c = concentration (mol dm⁻³)
→ absorbance ∝ concentration

Nucleic acid purity (A260/A280)

pure DNA ≈ 1.8 · pure RNA ≈ 2.0
DNA is lower because thymine (in DNA) has a lower 260/280 ratio than uracil (in RNA).

Applications

DNA quantification · protein quantification · carbohydrate estimation

  • draw an appropriate root cause — logical & scientific
  • collect historical evidence (same/other process in plant)
  • check if investigation must extend to past/future batches
  • give corrective action with evidence
  • suggest preventive action

4. Alert & action limits; positive & negative controls

Alert & action levels (EM) are NOT specifications — they must not be used for pass/fail of product. They are indicators of drift from the operating norm, judged by trend over time. Defined before the programme starts; reviewed (recommended annually) with a documented rationale.
  • Sterile mfg → levels from regulatory/compendial guidance. Other areas & alert levels → historical data review.
  • Investigation triggered by: result above action level · upward trend of alert excursions · frequency of incidents above a cut-off.
  • A single result rarely proves loss of control → trend is most important.

Why trend? — real isolator settle-plate data (50 days)

EM excursion investigation (general approach)

First rule out lab error (independently & quickly): sample taken/handled right? correct medium? sampler within calibration? correct incubation time/temp? contamination during testing? read correctly, right units?
Then: a) describe problem → b) examine trends (look back 3–6 months) → c) collect data → d) investigate → e) risk assessment (product) → f) most probable root cause → g) CAPA → h) summary.
Minimum preventive actions: extra cleaning/sanitisation, extra sampling, retrain operators. Same organism recurring → review disinfectant effectiveness/resistance.

Positive & negative controls: every media batch gets Growth Promotion Testing (GPT) — proves medium can grow organisms (guards against false negatives); validated sterilisation of media guards against false positives; keep live positive control cultures on dedicated pipettes, glassware & loops in their own zone.

5. Minimising cross-contamination, false positives & false negatives

False positive = growth/result shows contamination that wasn't really in the sample (introduced in the lab).
False negative = real contamination missed (medium/method failed to recover it).

5 control measures (slide)

  1. Environmental control — sterility & critical tests in certified LAF benches or Biosafety Cabinets (BSCs).
  2. Aseptic technique — strict; gowning qualification & periodic requalification.
  3. Media & reagent validation — GPT on every batch → prevents false negatives; validated sterilisation (autoclave/dry heat) → prevents false positives.
  4. Sanitisation regimen — rotate disinfectants (sporicides + alcohol) to prevent resistance; clean before & after every session.
  5. Equipment segregation — dedicated pipettes, glassware, loops per zone (esp. positive controls).

Causes of false POSITIVES

  1. Lab contamination — poor aseptic technique; contaminated pipettes, glassware, forceps
  2. EM errors — high airborne contamination during testing; contaminated settle/contact plates
  3. Media & reagents — non-sterile media; improper sterilisation; inadequate GPT
  4. Sampling & handling — contaminated containers; improper transfer; cross-contamination between samples
  5. Analytical/instrument — false signals from automated systems; poor calibration/thresholds; wrong colony counting, artefacts

Causes of false NEGATIVES (EM PDF)

  • media/incubation only grow organisms suited to chosen conditions
  • settle plates drying out (validate exposure time)
  • swabs recover less than contact plates
  • spraying hands before finger plates
  • disinfectant / fumigant residues → add neutralisers
  • no GPT; wrong incubation order; plates over-incubated & dried
  • particle loss: tubing > 3 m, non-isokinetic probe

Module VI — Test & Analysis of Incoming Materials (Biochemistry)

No PPT/PDF was given for this module. Everything below is pulled together from points in your other resources (Module 0.1, plant regulations, GLP slides, pharmacopoeia, tools PDF, OOS slides, MSDS slides, syllabus practicals) that match the Module VI topics.

1. Incoming material — from vendor to release

2. Sample handling & sampling per SOP

  • GLP key element: proper sample handling; label & store properly; record observations immediately.
  • Sampling problems that cause OOS (sample homogeneity): sampling error · handling of samples · pooling of sample.
  • False positives from contaminated sampling containers, improper collection/transfer, cross-contamination between samples.
  • Re-sampling only by QA, after QA head authorisation.
  • Sampling tools from notes: sterile swabs (defined area, pre-wetted), contact plates, settle plates, air samplers (micro); all in clean, labelled containers.

3. Weighing

  • Use an analytical balance — sub-milligram weighing; pan enclosed in a transparent cover so air currents & particles don't affect it; electromagnet balances the force.
  • Balance must be calibrated (GLP: calibrated & maintained equipment; IQ/OQ/PQ needs calibration first).
  • Weighing happens in the storage & weighing area, designed for orderly, clean handling.
  • Record weights at once (ALCOA — contemporaneous).

4. Good storage of samples & materials

  • Storage areas: clean, dry, within acceptable temperature limits; special conditions (temp, humidity) provided, checked & monitored.
  • Pharmacopoeia: suitable containers; protect from light, moisture & temperature; labels with name, strength, batch no., expiry, storage instructions.
  • Chemicals (MSDS): cool, dry, ventilated, away from sunlight, heat & ignition, away from oxidisers/incompatibles; keep closed & labelled; smallest quantity.
  • Long-term: deep freezer (very low temp → minimal microbial growth) for cultures, blood samples, medicines.
  • QC labs need storage space for samples, reference standards (with cooling if needed) & records.

5. Identifying & reporting nonconformity

  • Deviation = any departure from approved procedures or specifications → document & report.
  • Nonconforming test result = OOS → analyst recalculates → reports to QC in-charge → logs the OOS → investigation (Phase I → II → III, Module V).
  • Fix with CAPA; changes via change control; protocol failures in qualification → deviation handling (IQ/OQ/PQ).
  • Non-conforming material goes to the rejected area — kept separate from released stock.
